# Oliver Wyman Slide Design Selection Guide

## Purpose

This file teaches the Presentation Builder how to select an appropriate Oliver Wyman slide design based on communication intent, content structure, and expected audience behavior. It should be used together with the sample slide deck added to the Custom GPT knowledge base.

The builder should not treat the sample deck as a fixed template library only. It should learn the underlying design logic: each slide type solves a specific communication problem. The builder should first identify the purpose of the slide, then choose the layout that makes the message easiest to understand.

## Core design principles

### 1. Start with the message, not the layout

Before selecting a slide design, determine what the slide needs to do:

- Orient the audience
- Compare options
- Explain a process
- Show performance or impact
- Prioritize choices
- Structure a complex topic
- Humanize the story with imagery or a quote
- Provide detail for decision-making

Only after identifying the intent should the builder choose a slide design.

### 2. Use structure to reduce cognitive load

Oliver Wyman slides often use clear grids, dividers, columns, numbered elements, and restrained highlights. The builder should use these structures to help the reader scan quickly.

Avoid decorative complexity. Each visual device should clarify relationships between ideas.

### 3. Use big numbers only when they are the story

Large figures should be reserved for quantified proof points, performance deltas, market size, operational impact, or financial outcomes. If the numbers are supporting evidence rather than the main message, use a smaller table or text block instead.

### 4. Use visual hierarchy deliberately

A slide should have one clear entry point. Usually this is the title or a prominent number, image, matrix, or process structure. Supporting information should be visibly secondary.

### 5. Keep slide density appropriate to the decision moment

Use simpler slides for executive storytelling and denser slides for analysis, appendix material, or working sessions.

## Slide selection decision tree

Use this decision tree before creating a slide.

1. **Is the slide introducing, closing, or separating sections?**
   - Use: title slide, contents slide, divider/statement slide, quote slide.

2. **Is the slide explaining a sequence, process, journey, or maturity path?**
   - Use: chevrons, growing steps, timeline, circles and arrows.

3. **Is the slide comparing options, categories, plans, or packages?**
   - Use: two-column comparison, option comparison table, matrix.

4. **Is the slide proving impact with metrics?**
   - Use: big-number layouts, KPI rows, infographic with numbers, performance highlights overview.

5. **Is the slide organizing many related topics?**
   - Use: numbered topic list, column layouts, overview with table and heading.

6. **Is the slide explaining roles, governance, or team structure?**
   - Use: organizational chart or structured boxes.

7. **Is the slide meant to create emotional context or make the story more human?**
   - Use: text and image, big text and image, quote and image.

8. **Is the content too detailed for a storytelling slide?**
   - Use: table, overview, or appendix-style layout. Do not force detailed content into a highly visual slide.

## Slide design catalogue

### 1. Title slide

**Reference:** Slide 1

**Use when:**

- Starting a document or major deliverable
- Introducing a workshop, proposal, readout, or discussion document
- The audience needs simple orientation before the content begins

**Content pattern:**

- Document title
- Date or context line
- Optional client or project identifier if allowed

**Builder guidance:**

Use a calm, spacious layout. Do not add extra explanatory text unless required. The title should be direct and specific.

---

### 2. Numbered topic list with descriptions

**Reference:** Slide 2

**Use when:**

- Presenting 6-10 topics of equal importance
- Creating an agenda-like overview with short explanations
- Summarizing workstreams, hypotheses, risks, recommendations, or initiatives

**Best for:**

- Many items with similar weight
- Content that benefits from numbering
- Early orientation before deeper slides

**Avoid when:**

- Items need comparison or prioritization
- The list implies a process sequence with dependencies
- There are fewer than four items

**Builder guidance:**

Each item should have a short title and one concise description. Keep descriptions parallel in length and structure.

---

### 3. Two-row big-number comparison

**Reference:** Slide 3

**Use when:**

- Comparing two groups, time periods, markets, scenarios, or business units
- Showing multiple metrics across two rows
- Making performance differences easy to scan

**Best for:**

- 2 categories x 3 metrics
- Market sizing
- Financial comparison
- Before/after performance

**Avoid when:**

- The numbers require lengthy explanation
- There are more than three metrics per row
- The point is qualitative rather than quantitative

**Builder guidance:**

Make the row headers meaningful. Each number needs a short label or explanatory sentence. Do not use large numbers without context.

---

### 4. Three-column layout with dividers

**Reference:** Slide 4

**Use when:**

- Explaining three themes, pillars, findings, or implications
- Giving balanced space to three related ideas
- Combining short top summaries with deeper supporting text

**Best for:**

- Strategic pillars
- Three-part argument
- Three segments with similar detail
- Three recommendations with rationale

**Avoid when:**

- One column is clearly more important than the others
- The content is mainly numeric
- The text becomes too dense to scan

**Builder guidance:**

Use consistent column structure. Start each column with a concise heading, then add supporting text. Keep columns balanced.

---

### 5. Four-step process with chevrons

**Reference:** Slide 5

**Use when:**

- Showing a linear process with four stages
- Explaining a project approach, implementation path, operating model transition, or decision flow
- Making sequence and progression explicit

**Best for:**

- Step-by-step methodology
- Implementation phases
- Transformation roadmap at a high level
- Workplan logic before a detailed timeline

**Avoid when:**

- Steps happen in parallel
- The process has more than four major stages
- The main point is impact rather than method

**Builder guidance:**

Each step should have a short heading, a one-sentence description, and 2-4 bullets. Use verbs for step headings where possible.

---

### 6. Four text boxes with key message highlight

**Reference:** Slide 6

**Use when:**

- Presenting four related insight blocks
- Highlighting one message more prominently than the others
- Creating a modular summary slide with one callout

**Best for:**

- Four findings with one key implication
- Risk areas with one priority risk
- Four recommendations with one critical action

**Avoid when:**

- All four items need equal emphasis
- The content is a process or sequence
- The key message is too long

**Builder guidance:**

Use the highlighted box for the central takeaway, not just a fourth item. Keep all boxes concise.

---

### 7. Four-column layout with text and icons

**Reference:** Slide 7

**Use when:**

- Showing four capabilities, services, principles, behaviors, or components
- The audience benefits from quick visual recognition through icons
- Each item is conceptually distinct but similar in importance

**Best for:**

- Service offerings
- Capability model
- Value drivers
- Design principles

**Avoid when:**

- The icons would be decorative rather than meaningful
- The items need ranking or sequencing
- Descriptions are long

**Builder guidance:**

Use simple, consistent icons. Each column should include one short heading and a concise explanation. Do not over-explain.

---

### 8. Four-column layout with text and numbers

**Reference:** Slide 8

**Use when:**

- Showing four ordered points, priorities, stages, or modules
- Numbers help the audience follow sequence or reference items in discussion
- Text is short and structured

**Best for:**

- Four recommendations
- Four workstreams
- Four design rules
- Four decision criteria

**Avoid when:**

- Numbers imply sequence but the content is not sequential
- More than four items are needed
- The content requires icons or data visualization

**Builder guidance:**

Numbering should add meaning. Use it for sequence, ranking, or easy reference. Keep descriptions short.

---

### 9. Services and performance highlights overview

**Reference:** Slide 9

**Use when:**

- Combining service/category coverage with evidence of experience or impact
- Showing capabilities, industries, and proof points in one executive overview
- Presenting credentials or relevant experience

**Best for:**

- Proposal credentials
- Capability overview
- Practice or team introduction
- Case experience summary

**Avoid when:**

- The audience needs a single clear recommendation
- The information is not strongly related
- There are too many categories to scan

**Builder guidance:**

Use the table to show coverage and the right-side numbers to prove credibility. Keep labels short. Make sure the performance highlights are specific and evidence-based.

---

### 10. Overview with table and heading

**Reference:** Slide 10

**Use when:**

- Pairing a narrative overview with structured data
- Introducing a topic and providing a supporting table on the same slide
- Giving the audience both context and detail

**Best for:**

- Project overview with scope table
- Workstream summary
- Stakeholder or milestone list
- Initial analysis summary

**Avoid when:**

- The table is too wide or too dense
- The narrative and table are unrelated
- The slide needs to persuade quickly

**Builder guidance:**

Place the message on the left and the structured evidence on the right. The table should support the narrative directly.

---

### 11. Project overview with divider and KPI blocks

**Reference:** Slide 11

**Use when:**

- Explaining a multi-part program with measurable results
- Combining method, people/change, rapid capability building, and economics
- Showing qualitative actions alongside KPI outcomes

**Best for:**

- Transformation case story
- Operational improvement summary
- Program impact readout
- Turnaround example

**Avoid when:**

- The story can be told with one or two metrics
- The slide is for a senior executive who needs only the headline
- The content lacks strong KPI evidence

**Builder guidance:**

Use this layout for dense but structured case-style information. Keep each block focused on one theme. Put the strongest KPI results where the eye lands first.

---

### 12. Three columns with big numbers

**Reference:** Slide 12

**Use when:**

- Leading with 2-4 major metrics and explaining the meaning underneath
- Showing business impact supported by narrative detail
- Making quantitative proof the main story

**Best for:**

- Savings impact
- Margin impact
- Time reduction
- Operational performance improvement

**Avoid when:**

- The numbers are weak, unclear, or not comparable
- The text below is too long
- The slide needs a visual process or table instead

**Builder guidance:**

Use large metrics sparingly and with confidence. Each number should answer: “So what?” Add a short label directly under or beside each metric.

---

### 13. 2x2 matrix with comments

**Reference:** Slide 13

**Use when:**

- Prioritizing options across two dimensions
- Mapping initiatives by complexity, impact, value, urgency, readiness, risk, or feasibility
- Showing strategic choices with commentary

**Best for:**

- Portfolio prioritization
- Initiative sequencing
- Market or segment assessment
- Product feature evaluation

**Avoid when:**

- The axes are not clear or measurable
- There are more than 8-10 items
- The conclusion is obvious without the matrix

**Builder guidance:**

Use meaningful axis labels. Add short comments that explain implications, not just placement. Highlight the most attractive or urgent quadrant when appropriate.

---

### 14. Two-column comparison

**Reference:** Slide 14

**Use when:**

- Comparing two options, models, scenarios, or strategic choices
- The audience needs to understand trade-offs
- A simple “Option 1 vs. Option 2” structure is enough

**Best for:**

- Build vs. buy
- Current vs. future state
- Centralized vs. decentralized model
- Two solution paths

**Avoid when:**

- There are more than two options
- Detailed feature-level comparison is required
- The options need scoring or weighting

**Builder guidance:**

Make the two columns structurally parallel. Use matched criteria where possible. Avoid making one side much longer than the other unless the imbalance is intentional.

---

### 15. Five growing steps

**Reference:** Slide 15

**Use when:**

- Showing progression, maturity, scale-up, or increasing ambition
- Explaining how an organization moves from current state to target state
- Showing a gradual build from start to end

**Best for:**

- Maturity model
- Capability ramp-up
- Adoption journey
- Implementation escalation

**Avoid when:**

- The steps are not cumulative
- The sequence is not important
- The story has fewer than four stages

**Builder guidance:**

Each step should represent a meaningful increase in maturity, scope, scale, or impact. The final step should clearly feel like the target state.

---

### 16. 5x5 matrix for two drivers

**Reference:** Slide 16

**Use when:**

- Segmenting opportunities, initiatives, or decisions across two graded drivers
- Showing distribution across low, medium, and high zones
- Creating a more nuanced version of a 2x2 matrix

**Best for:**

- Impact vs. time horizon
- Risk vs. value
- Readiness vs. benefit
- Complexity vs. payoff

**Avoid when:**

- There are only a few items
- The data is not reliable enough for granular placement
- The audience needs a fast conclusion

**Builder guidance:**

Use this layout only when granularity matters. Label the axes clearly and avoid overfilling the grid.

---

### 17. Diagram with circles and arrows

**Reference:** Slide 17

> **Runtime note:** `show_cycle` renders this as a ring of ovals with arrows, so
> it implies a **cycle** (a loop where the last item feeds back to the first).
> Use it only for genuinely cyclical or iterative relationships. Do **not** use it
> to display a set of parallel, non-sequential topics (e.g. a list of priorities or
> workstreams) — that misrepresents them as a loop. For parallel topics use a
> multi-column layout or `dashboard` cards instead.

**Use when:**

- Explaining iterative loops or recurring processes (the steps repeat)
- Showing a feedback cycle where the end returns to the start

**Best for:**

- Operating model logic
- Workflow concept
- Ecosystem or interaction model
- Continuous improvement cycle

**Avoid when:**

- The sequence is strictly linear and better suited to chevrons
- There are many detailed dependencies
- The arrows do not add meaning

**Builder guidance:**

Use arrows only to show meaningful movement or dependency. Keep circle labels short. Do not make the diagram decorative.

---

### 18. Infographic with three elements and numbers

**Reference:** Slide 18

**Use when:**

- Combining a short narrative with three supporting metrics
- Showing three proof points that reinforce one story
- Creating a visually balanced insight slide

**Best for:**

- Customer insight with three statistics
- Impact summary
- Research findings
- Performance snapshot

**Avoid when:**

- There are more than three metrics
- The numbers are not comparable or thematically linked
- The slide needs a table for precision

**Builder guidance:**

Start with a short narrative statement, then use the three numbers as evidence. Each number should have one clear explanation.

---

### 19. Organizational chart with boxes

**Reference:** Slide 19

**Use when:**

- Showing reporting lines, governance, project team structure, or decision rights
- Explaining roles and accountability
- Mapping people or functions in a hierarchy

**Best for:**

- Governance model
- Project organization
- Leadership structure
- Account team overview

**Avoid when:**

- The structure is network-based rather than hierarchical
- Roles are still uncertain
- The slide needs to explain behaviors rather than structure

**Builder guidance:**

Keep names and titles short. Group related roles visually. Do not overload with responsibilities unless the layout is adapted.

---

### 20. Table comparing options

**Reference:** Slide 20

**Use when:**

- Comparing multiple packages, features, tiers, vendors, scenarios, or options
- The audience needs detailed side-by-side evaluation
- Binary indicators, checks, crosses, or price rows help decision-making

**Best for:**

- Product tier comparison
- Vendor evaluation
- Offer/package comparison
- Feature matrix

**Avoid when:**

- Only two options are compared; use two-column comparison instead
- The audience needs a quick strategic takeaway
- The table has too many rows to read comfortably

**Builder guidance:**

Use this layout when detail matters. Make the evaluation criteria clear and consistent. Highlight the preferred option only if the decision is clear.

### 21. Timeline

**Reference:** Slide 21

**Use when:**

- Showing milestones across years, quarters, months, or phases
- Explaining timing, sequencing, and dependencies
- Mapping what happens when

**Best for:**

- Roadmap
- Implementation schedule
- Program phases
- Historical progression

**Avoid when:**

- The sequence is conceptual rather than time-based
- The dates are uncertain
- The timeline has too many milestones

**Builder guidance:**

Use time labels consistently. Place milestones above and below the line to avoid clutter. Keep each milestone description short.

### 22. Big text and image

**Reference:** Slide 22

**Use when:**

- Creating a more editorial, human, or story-led page
- Pairing a strong visual with one or two narrative blocks
- Opening a section with atmosphere and context

**Best for:**

- Section opener
- Strategic narrative
- Client context
- Industry environment

**Avoid when:**

- The slide needs detailed comparison or data
- The image is generic or unrelated
- The text becomes too long

**Builder guidance:**

Choose imagery that supports the message. The text should be punchy and structured, not a full analysis block.

### 23. Big text with three columns and image

- The three columns need extensive text

### 24. Text and image

**Reference:** Slide 24

**Reference:** Slide 26

**Reference:** Slide 27

## Density guidance

### Low-density slides

Use for executive storytelling, section transitions, and key messages.

Examples:

- Title slide
- Big text and image
- Text and image
- Quote and image
- Large statement slide

### Medium-density slides

Use for core presentation flow where the audience needs both structure and substance.

Examples:

- Four-column layouts
- Three-column layout
- Chevrons
- Five growing steps
- Infographic with numbers
- 2x2 matrix

### High-density slides

Use for evidence, decision support, appendix, or working sessions.

Examples:

- Overview with table and heading
- Services and performance highlights overview
- Project overview with divider and KPI blocks
- Table comparing options
- 5x5 matrix
- Organizational chart

## Builder rules for selecting layouts

### Rule 1: Match the visual metaphor to the content

- Sequence → chevrons, timeline, growing steps
- Comparison → two-column layout, table, matrix
- Evidence → big numbers, KPI blocks, performance highlights
- Structure → columns, topic list, org chart
- Emotion/context → image or quote slide

### Rule 2: Do not use a table when the story is a headline

If the main point can be expressed as one clear metric or comparison, use a big-number slide instead of a table.

### Rule 3: Do not use big numbers when the numbers are secondary

If metrics are supporting evidence only, place them inside a structured overview or table rather than making them the slide’s primary visual element.

### Rule 4: Use matrices only when two drivers matter

A matrix needs two meaningful axes. If there is only one ranking dimension, use a list, table, or steps instead.

### Rule 5: Use process visuals only for real progression

Chevrons, timelines, arrows, and growing steps imply movement. Do not use them for unrelated categories.

### Rule 6: Use images only when they carry meaning

Images should provide context, emotion, industry setting, or human relevance. Avoid generic decorative imagery.

### Rule 7: Preserve parallel structure

When using columns, option comparisons, tables, or steps, each section should follow the same pattern. This makes the slide easier to scan.

## Prompt instructions for the Presentation Builder

When building a presentation, follow this sequence:

1. Read the user’s content and identify the communication intent of each slide.
2. Classify each slide as orientation, context, analysis, comparison, process, recommendation, implementation, evidence, or closing.
3. Select the matching slide design from the sample deck.
4. Preserve the structural logic of the reference slide, but adapt labels and content to the user’s topic.
5. Keep headings concise and specific.
6. Use numbers, tables, matrices, and images only when they improve understanding.
7. Avoid forcing content into a visual format that changes its meaning.
8. Prefer fewer, clearer slides over one overloaded slide.

---

## Output expectations

For each generated slide, the builder should be able to state:

- The selected slide design
- The reference slide number from the sample deck
- The reason for choosing it
- The intended communication purpose
- The required content fields
- Any content that should be shortened, split, or moved to appendix

Example output format:

```text
Slide design: 4-step process – chevrons
Reference: Sample slide 5
Purpose: Explain a sequential implementation approach
Why this design: The content describes four dependent stages that should be read left to right
Required fields: Step heading, one-sentence description, 2-4 bullets per step
Content caution: Do not include detailed milestones; use a timeline slide if dates are required
```

## Quality checklist

Before finalizing a slide, check:

- Does the layout match the slide’s communication purpose?
- Is there one clear main message?
- Is the hierarchy obvious within three seconds?
- Are columns, rows, or steps balanced?
- Are numbers explained with meaningful labels?
- Are matrices using meaningful axes?
