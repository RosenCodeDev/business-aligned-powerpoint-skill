# Content Structures

Canonical deck shapes for common strategy deliverables. This file governs the
**horizontal** structure of a deck — which kind of deliverable it is and the slide
sequence that deliverable usually follows. It is applied between source analysis
(Phase 3) and the outline (Phase 4), and pairs with `15_storyline_logic.md`, which
then builds the argument inside the chosen shape.

## The one rule that overrides everything here

These archetypes **organize what is already in the sources. They do not generate
analysis.** If the chosen shape has a slide the source material cannot support —
a profit pool with no margin data, a business case with no numbers, competitor
moves with no competitor intelligence — flag the gap to the user and either drop
that slide or ask them to supply the input. Never fabricate market sizes, figures,
competitor behaviour, or findings to complete a shape. A deck that admits a gap is
correct; a deck that invents authoritative-looking analysis is a failure, even if
it looks polished and on-brand.

## How a shape gets chosen

1. **Infer and propose (default).** After Phase 3, read what the sources actually
   contain and propose the matching archetype in plain language at the start of
   Phase 4 — e.g. "your material reads as a market landscape plus a recommendation;
   I'll structure it as Strategic Options unless you'd prefer otherwise." The user
   confirms or redirects. This rides on the existing outline approval loop; it adds
   no question to the Phase 1 interview.
2. **Explicit override (optional lever).** If the user named a shape in their
   opening brief ("structure this as a business case"), that wins over inference.
3. **Mixed decks.** Real decks often combine archetypes — e.g. a Situation
   Assessment section feeding a Strategic Options section. Compose them in sequence
   rather than forcing a single label.

The chosen shape sets the slide *sequence*; `15_storyline_logic.md` then sets the
*argument*; the design layer (`21_slide_design_selection_guide.md`) picks the visual
treatment for each slide. Keep those three jobs separate.

## Archetype catalogue

Each archetype lists when it applies and its canonical sequence. Treat the sequence
as a default to adapt, not a fixed template.

### 1. Situation Assessment
Applies when the deck must establish where things stand before any move is chosen.
Sequence: context and scope → fact base / current performance → momentum read
(what's improving, what's eroding) → key issues → the question to resolve next.

### 2. Market & Competitive Landscape
Applies when the case depends on the playing field — market size, segments, white
space, and how rivals behave.
Sequence: market definition and size → segmentation / where value sits → competitor
map and likely moves → white space or attractive positions → implications for us.

### 3. Strategic Options & Recommendation
Applies when leaders need to choose a path. The most common executive deck shape.
Sequence: the decision and why now → the option set (kept genuinely distinct) →
evaluation criteria → option-by-option assessment → recommendation → what it takes.

### 4. Business Case
Applies when a specific move needs its economics defended.
Sequence: the move and its rationale → assumptions → base-case economics →
sensitivities / scenarios → key risks → recommendation and decision ask.
(Requires real numbers in the sources — otherwise flag and stop.)

### 5. Operating Model & Execution Design
Applies when an agreed strategy must become how work gets done.
Sequence: what the strategy demands → capabilities required → structure and decision
rights → ways of working / governance → gaps from today → transition implications.

### 6. Transformation Roadmap
Applies when a strategy must become sequenced delivery. Very common as a closing
section.
Sequence: the end-state ambition → workstreams → phased timeline → owners and
milestones → dependencies and first-90-days → how progress is governed.

### 7. Risk & Mitigation
Often a section rather than a whole deck. Applies when a recommendation needs to be
pressure-tested.
Sequence: the principal risks → likelihood and impact → mitigations and owners →
residual risk → triggers to watch.

### 8. Value Realization & Metrics
Applies when benefits must be tracked after a decision or launch.
Sequence: the value at stake → the few decision-linked KPIs → baselines and targets
→ tracking cadence and ownership → what action each metric triggers.

## Note on naming

These shapes draw on well-established strategy communication structures (pyramid
reasoning, options analysis, SCQA). Keep Oliver Wyman's own naming and voice in all
output; do not surface any third-party consultancy's branding in the deck.
